📊 Demographics
Racial composition of the Pullman neighborhood over time
Neighborhood history
1880s
Pullman opens as a planned company town. Workers are recruited from Northern Europe — Swedish, German, and Dutch immigrants fill the rowhouses. The neighborhood is essentially all white by design.
1910s–40s
The Great Migration brings hundreds of thousands of Black families to Chicago's South Side. Racially restrictive covenants keep Pullman off-limits. Neighboring Roseland integrates while Pullman stays almost entirely white.
1955–60
Pullman Car Works ends manufacturing operations. The Calumet industrial corridor — steel mills, rail yards, meatpacking — begins a long decline. Longtime white residents start leaving for the suburbs, aided by FHA loans available only to them.
1960s
Interstate 94 cuts through the South Side, accelerating neighborhood fracture. Black families from Roseland and Woodlawn move into Pullman as covenants fall. The neighborhood goes from nearly all white to majority Black within a decade. Residents begin organizing to protect the historic buildings from urban renewal demolition.
1970–72
The Pullman Civic Organization, led by Americo Lisciotto, mobilizes against city plans to demolish the rowhouses. Their campaign succeeds: Pullman is listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1970 and becomes a Chicago Landmark in 1972 — protecting more than 900 original structures during the neighborhood's most vulnerable decade.
1973–80
The Historic Pullman Foundation is established to steward the district. Restoration of Hotel Florence begins. Meanwhile, Wisconsin Steel closes in 1980, eliminating 3,500 jobs overnight. South Works and other Calumet mills follow. Unemployment spikes across the Far South Side.
1980s–90s
Disinvestment deepens — banks redline the area, businesses close, population drops. Chicago begins demolishing the high-rise public housing projects on the lakefront — Robert Taylor Homes, Stateway Gardens. Many displaced residents relocate to Far South Side neighborhoods, increasing pressure on already-strained communities.
2000s
The Pullman House Project begins restoring individual rowhouses and opening them for tours. Community-led efforts preserve the physical fabric of the district even as economic recovery lags. The neighborhood stabilizes around its historic identity.
2015–now
President Obama designates Pullman a National Monument — the first in Illinois. Method Products opens on the old Wisconsin Steel site, the first major industrial employer in decades. The National Park Service takes stewardship of the core historic buildings. Slow reinvestment follows, anchored by decades of community organizing that kept the neighborhood intact.
Sources: U.S. Census Bureau decennial census; CMAP Community Data Snapshots; Big Shoulders Atlas (2011). Figures reflect the Pullman community area (tracts 5001–5003). The national park boundary covers a subset of this area.
